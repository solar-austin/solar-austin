Detailed Methodology, Assumptions, and Sources

Austin Energy has an adopted 2035 direction to move toward a carbon-free portfolio in its Resource, Generation and Climate Protection Plan to 2035, but it has not published a fully specified project-by-project implementation plan with complete build schedules, fixed procurement outcomes, and unit-by-unit retirement dates. This model should therefore be read as an Austin Energy-specific scenario tool built from the best public data available, with clear distinctions between Austin Energy source data, ERCOT shape proxies, national reference values, and explicit planning assumptions.

This model combines three linked views of the same scenario: an annual energy outlook from 2025 through 2035, a 24-hour 2035 summer reliability stress test, and a 2035 landed-cost table. The numbers here are meant to make the model transparent. Some inputs are tied to public Austin Energy or ERCOT data, while others are explicit planning assumptions chosen in the model and kept editable in the UI.

1. Horizon, units, and baseline structure

The planning horizon is 11 years, from 2025 through 2035. Annual energy is modeled in TWh, reliability is modeled hour by hour in MW and MWh, and costs are shown in $/MWh, $k/MW-year for batteries, and total annual cost in millions of dollars. The baseline 2025 annual load is 14.2 TWh. The baseline summer peak for the reliability stress test is 3,135 MW, based on Austin Energy's reported summer 2024 peak demand. The load-growth slider applies to both the annual energy outlook and the 2035 stress test.

Annual load\( \mathrm{Load}(i) = 14.2 \times (1 + \mathrm{growth}/100)^i \)
2035 stress-day peak\( \mathrm{Peak}_{2035} = 3135 \times (1 + \mathrm{growth}/100)^{10} \)

1a. Initial conditions and starting fleet

The model's starting point is a mix of current Austin Energy fleet values and planning defaults. Current-fleet defaults are used where Austin Energy has a clearly published resource quantity: nuclear starts at 430 MW (Austin Energy's STP share), biomass starts at 105 MW (Nacogdoches), utility wind starts at about 1,796 MW, utility battery starts at 100 MW based on Austin Energy's announced 2025 utility-scale battery deal, and gas baseload/peaker start at 595 MW and 205 MW. These are meant to approximate today's published Austin Energy portfolio and local thermal fleet rather than an optimized 2035 endpoint.

Not every default is a pure current-fleet snapshot. Utility solar in the model still defaults to the surviving existing utility-solar portfolio used in the contract-retirement logic, and local solar defaults to a planning target path rather than today's installed level. Local battery remains at 0 MW by default because a clear current Austin Energy public aggregate for customer-sited battery capacity was not identified. So the starting conditions are best read as a transparent hybrid baseline: current Austin Energy where public quantities are clear, plus explicit planning assumptions where they are not.

2a. 24-hour reliability approach

The 24-hour reliability chart uses a different lens. It asks what output may be available hour by hour on a hot August day, rather than how much energy a resource produces over a full year. For example, a 100 MW solar resource does not produce 100 MW every hour of the day. It may be near zero before sunrise, rise toward midday, and then fall back toward zero in the evening. The daily chart therefore uses hourly availability profiles instead of annual energy factors. Wind works the same way: the question is how much wind output is likely to be available in each hour, not what its annual average happens to be.

The exact hourly load, wind, solar, and import-support distributions used in that stress test are described later in Sections 8 and 9. This section is only meant to distinguish the daily reliability lens from the annual and monthly energy views.

Reliability reshaping\( \mathrm{Hourly\ Value}(h) = \mathrm{Nameplate\ MW} \times \mathrm{Hourly\ Profile}(h) \)

2b. Import allowance in the reliability model

The model includes an Import Allowance input to represent outside power that Austin Energy can plausibly rely on in stressed hours. This is not treated as owned generation. It is a capped market-support layer that sits after local generation, battery discharge, and gas peakers. In the charts, it appears separately from Austin Energy resources so imported support is visually distinct from owned or contracted supply.

The current default is 700 MW. This should be read as an effective stressed-hour import allowance, not as a literal transmission rating. In reality, Austin's usable outside support depends on both transmission into the load zone and whether ERCOT has power available to deliver during a tight hour. The model compresses those effects into one editable allowance so the user can test how much outside support is assumed to be dependable.

The 700 MW default is a calibrated planning placeholder rather than a published Austin Energy transfer limit. It was chosen because Austin Energy and related city materials point to the same general scale: a recent Austin transmission discussion described roughly a 700 MW local generation gap after Decker steam-unit retirements, while the Round II portfolio materials treated a +250 MW import-capacity increase as a meaningful but incremental reliability improvement in portfolios 14-16. In other words, the model uses 700 MW as a reasonable stressed-hour outside-support baseline that is large enough to matter, but still small enough that a further 250 MW increase changes outcomes. Sources: EUC transmission discussion and Round II Modeling Results.

Import dispatch\( \mathrm{Imports}(h) = \min(\mathrm{Import\ Allowance}, \mathrm{Shortfall\ After\ Local\ Resources}(h)) \)
Residual risk\( \mathrm{Residual\ Shortfall}(h) = \max(0, \mathrm{Load}(h) - \mathrm{Local\ Supply}(h) - \mathrm{Imports}(h)) \)

In the annual and monthly charts, the same allowance is shown as a capped market-support layer ahead of any residual deficit. In the peak-day chart, it is the last supply layer used before an hour counts as residual deficit or risk.

3. Where the default price assumptions come from

The default generation-price assumptions in the landed-cost table are based on midpoint values from Lazard's June 2025 LCOE+ Version 18.0 where the model has a reasonably close technology match. Battery defaults are based on Lazard's June 2025 LCOS Version 10.0 utility-scale standalone 4-hour storage range in $/kW-year, referenced from Lazard's 2025 LCOE+ overview page. Geothermal is treated separately: because an Austin-focused buildout would likely rely on deeper EGS-style geothermal rather than conventional western hydrothermal, the model uses a higher planning placeholder informed by NREL's 2025 Geothermal Market Report, which says EGS remains on a declining path toward hydrothermal-like costs rather than already matching them today. Local solar and local battery are treated differently from utility-side resources: their default utility cost is set to zero because they are modeled as private purchases rather than Austin Energy spending. These are still planning defaults for scenario testing, not Austin Energy's actual contract prices, and every value remains editable in the table.

The current default model values are: nuclear $34/MWh, biomass $0/MWh placeholder, gas baseload $32/MWh, gas peaker $109/MWh, coal $73/MWh, existing wind $62/MWh, new wind $62/MWh, existing solar $58/MWh, new solar $58/MWh, local solar $0/MWh, geothermal $140/MWh, utility battery $238k/MW-year, and local battery $0k/MW-year. The model also keeps energy efficiency at $25/MWh and demand response at $25/MWh. A default TCOS adder of $40/MWh is applied to eligible remote resources unless overridden in the table.

Landed cost\( \mathrm{Landed\ Cost} = \mathrm{Base\ Price} + \mathrm{TCOS} \)
Energy resource cost\( \mathrm{Annual\ Cost\ (\$M)} = \mathrm{TWh} \times \mathrm{Landed\ \$/MWh} \)
Battery cost\( \mathrm{Annual\ Cost\ (\$M)} = \dfrac{\mathrm{MW} \times (\mathrm{\$k/MW\text{-}yr})}{1000} \)

The current Lazard mapping used in the model is: gas baseload to operating gas combined cycle, gas peaker to operating gas peaking, nuclear to operating U.S. nuclear, coal to operating coal, utility wind to onshore wind, utility solar to utility solar PV, and utility battery to utility-scale standalone 4-hour LCOS. Geothermal is not using the generic Lazard geothermal row; it uses a higher deep-geothermal planning placeholder informed by NREL's geothermal market report. Local solar and local battery are intentionally excluded from Austin Energy utility-side cost totals by default, so their base-cost defaults are zero unless the user chooses to model a utility-side incentive or program cost explicitly.

3a. Buy/sell market price simulation

The model now treats market purchases and market sales as time-shaped rather than flat annual prices. This matters because a resource can look cheap on annual energy alone while still creating an unfavorable trading pattern over the year. A solar-heavy portfolio, for example, may export more power in sunny midday hours when sale values tend to be weaker, while still importing during late-day and high-load hours when purchase values tend to be stronger.

The current implementation uses actual 2025 ERCOT Austin load-zone day-ahead prices averaged into a 12-by-24 month-hour matrix. For each month and hour, the model compares supply to load, prices imports at that month's average Austin load-zone price for that hour, and prices exports at the same market price for that hour. That means a solar-heavy portfolio can still earn less on exports if those exports land in lower-priced midday cells, while a short portfolio can still pay more if its imports land in stronger late-day or summer cells. Sources: ERCOT Market Prices, ERCOT DAM Settlement Point Prices Display, and ERCOT Real-Time Settlement Point Prices Display.

Hourly net market position\( \mathrm{Net}(m,h) = \mathrm{Load}(m,h) - \mathrm{Supply}(m,h) \)
Market purchase cost\( \sum_{m,h} \max(0,\mathrm{Net}(m,h)) \times \mathrm{BuyPrice}(m,h) \)
Market sale revenue\( \sum_{m,h} \max(0,-\mathrm{Net}(m,h)) \times \mathrm{SellPrice}(m,h) \)

4. Existing wind retirements and contract end dates

Existing utility wind is modeled from a project list with MW and contract-end years. The rule used in the code is simple: a project is treated as active through its listed expiration year and drops out beginning the following year. This means the model does not phase down a project inside its final contract year; it remains fully available through that year and then retires all at once in the next year.

These project-level assumptions come from Austin Energy planning materials, especially the Austin Energy 2035 planning appendix and related City of Austin 2035 plan materials.

6. How 2035 build targets are turned into annual additions

The sliders are interpreted as 2035 end-state targets. The model then works backward to determine the constant annual build needed to reach that 2035 level. There are nine modeled build years in the current logic. New build tranches are assumed to start producing the year after they are built, which creates a one-year lag in the annual outlook.

For utility wind, the model subtracts surviving existing wind in 2035 from the selected 2035 wind target, then spreads the remaining requirement evenly over the build years. For utility solar, the model subtracts surviving existing solar in 2035 and then adjusts the annual build calculation to account for solar degradation across build vintages. For local solar, the baseline starts at 188 MW and only additions above that level are spread across build years. Geothermal starts from zero. Utility battery and local battery are treated as additions above the current baseline/default settings.

New wind needed\( \max(0,\ \mathrm{wind\ target}_{2035} - \mathrm{surviving\ existing\ wind}_{2035}) \)
Wind annual build\( \mathrm{Wind\ Annual\ Build} = \dfrac{\mathrm{new\ wind\ needed}}{\mathrm{build\ years}} \)
New utility solar needed\( \max(0,\ \mathrm{solar\ target}_{2035} - \mathrm{surviving\ existing\ solar}_{2035}) \)
Solar annual build\( \mathrm{Solar\ Annual\ Build} = \dfrac{\mathrm{new\ solar\ needed}}{\sum(\mathrm{degradation\text{-}adjusted\ vintage\ factors})} \)
Local solar annual build\( \mathrm{Local\ Solar\ Annual\ Build} = \dfrac{\mathrm{target}_{2035} - 188}{\mathrm{build\ years}} \)

7. Seasonal distributions used in the charts

The monthly seasonality used in the top chart's optional seasonal mode and in the 2035 monthly chart is split by resource type. Load seasonality currently uses an ERCOT-wide monthly proxy derived from monthly Net Energy for Load, averaging the 2024 and 2025 actual values from ERCOT's monthly demand and energy workbook. Wind seasonality was derived from 2025 ERCOT monthly generation by fuel in ERCOT's monthly generation-by-fuel workbook. Monthly solar seasonality still uses the Austin solar-geometry method: the model evaluates a representative mid-month day for each month, sums the 24-hour solar shape, and then normalizes the resulting monthly factors so the hours-weighted annual average equals 1.0. That means the seasonal factors reshape the year while preserving the annual total.

Monthly shaping\( \mathrm{Monthly\ Value} = \mathrm{Annual\ Average\ Value} \times \mathrm{Normalized\ Monthly\ Factor} \)

That means monthly load and wind remain ERCOT-based shape proxies, while monthly solar uses an Austin-geometry shape. The selected-year daily stress test uses a separate hourly method described in Section 8.

8. Daily distributions used in the selected-year reliability stress test

The reliability chart is still a single representative 24-hour summer stress test, not a full production-cost model and not a full year of hourly simulations. The peak-load level uses Austin Energy's reported summer 2024 peak demand of 3,135 MW as the base value, grown forward with the model's load-growth slider to the year selected above the chart. That 3,135 MW value is treated as the peak-hour load anchor, not as a day-average level. The hourly load shape itself uses an ERCOT proxy from ERCOT's 2025 hourly native load archive. The model identifies the top 10 August 2025 ERCOT peak-load days, averages each hour across those days, and then normalizes the resulting 24-hour load series so its own maximum equals 1.0 before applying the Austin Energy peak. That means the reliability chart uses an Austin Energy peak-load level with an ERCOT hourly shape proxy.

Solar is no longer using the ERCOT operational solar curve in this daily stress test. Utility solar now uses a capacity-weighted Austin Energy fleet profile built from the published utility-solar project list and approximate project locations. The model computes an August solar-geometry curve for each site, combines those curves by project MW, and uses the resulting location-weighted fleet bell curve directly. Local solar uses an Austin-centered August solar-geometry curve. This produces a broader bell-curve-style daily solar shape that is tied to Austin Energy's own asset geography rather than ERCOT's aggregate operational fleet behavior. Source: Austin Energy renewable project list.

The model treats nuclear, biomass, baseload gas, coal, and geothermal as flat within the day. Wind still uses the ERCOT planning-profile-based August hourly availability curve, while solar now uses Austin Energy-specific geometry for utility assets and Austin-local geometry for local solar. So the daily chart mixes ERCOT-derived wind availability with Austin Energy-specific solar availability.

Hourly load\( \mathrm{Load}(h) = \mathrm{Peak}_{2035} \times \mathrm{load\ profile}(h) \)
Hourly utility solar\( \mathrm{Utility\ Solar}(h) = \mathrm{utility\ solar\ MW} \times \mathrm{AE\ fleet\ solar\ profile}(h) \)
Hourly local solar\( \mathrm{Local\ Solar}(h) = \mathrm{local\ solar\ MW} \times \mathrm{Austin\ local\ solar\ profile}(h) \)
Hourly wind\( \mathrm{Wind}(h) = \mathrm{wind\ MW} \times \mathrm{Aug\ Wind\ Availability}(h) \)

9. Battery treatment

Batteries are modeled as four-hour storage. In other words, every 1 MW of battery adds 4 MWh of storage energy capacity in the reliability model. The model uses a simple state-of-charge simulation to charge during surplus hours and discharge during deficit hours. It does not currently include round-trip efficiency losses, battery degradation, or detailed charging constraints tied to a specific charging source. The default utility-scale battery baseline is 100 MW, based on Austin Energy's announced 2025 battery storage deal. The default local-battery baseline remains 0 MW because a current public Austin Energy aggregate for customer-sited battery capacity was not identified.

Battery duration\( \mathrm{Energy\ Capacity} = \mathrm{Battery\ MW} \times 4\ \mathrm{hours} \)

In the cost model, batteries are not priced on annual TWh output. They are priced as annual capacity-style resources using $k/MW-year.

10. Gap, surplus, and reliability margin treatment

Whenever annual supply is below annual load, the difference is shown as a deficit or market gap. In the 2035 landed-cost table and buildout table, that deficit is priced through the ERCOT Austin load-zone month-hour market matrix plus any applicable TCOS. If annual supply is above annual load, the model shows surplus sales revenue using the same market matrix. In the reliability chart, risk hours are hours where supply falls short of load by more than 10 MW. The margin KPI uses the minimum hourly value of (supply minus load) divided by load.

Annual gap\( \max(0,\ \mathrm{annual\ load} - \mathrm{annual\ supply}) \)
Risk hour\( \mathrm{Hourly\ Load} > \mathrm{Hourly\ Supply} + 10\ \mathrm{MW} \)
Margin\( \mathrm{Margin\ \%} = \min_h\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{supply}(h)-\mathrm{load}(h)}{\mathrm{load}(h)}\right)\times 100 \)

11. Important interpretation notes

The model is intentionally transparent and scenario-oriented. It is not a full production-cost model, not a full capacity-expansion model, and not a utility budgeting system. The cost table uses editable planning assumptions. The monthly profiles use ERCOT-level proxies. The reliability test uses one representative day rather than many weather years. No specific Austin Energy gas-plant retirement dates between 2025 and 2036 are hard-coded; the model only reflects the user-selected gas levels plus the broader public planning context in Austin Energy's 2035 plan materials and current power plants overview.
